# Extended Technology Readiness Scale

## Standard NASA TRL Scale (1-9)

| TRL | Definition | Description | Exit Criteria |
|-----|-----------|-------------|---------------|
| 9 | System proven in operations | Fully integrated, successful mission operations | Operational reports |
| 8 | System qualified through test | Flight qualified, ready for implementation | Test & evaluation reports |
| 7 | Prototype demonstrated in operational environment | Working model in space/operational environment | Results from prototype |
| 6 | System prototype in relevant environment | Fully functional prototype or representational model | Results from lab testing |
| 5 | Breadboard validated in relevant environment | Rigorous testing of integrated components | Results from testing |
| 4 | Components validated in lab | Multiple components tested together | Test results |
| 3 | Proof of concept | Analytical and laboratory studies demonstrate viability | Lab results |
| 2 | Concept formulated | Application identified but speculative; no experimental proof | Documented concept |
| 1 | Basic principles observed | Scientific research beginning; results translated to R&D | Peer-reviewed publication |

## Drexler's Exploratory Engineering

Sits at approximately TRL 1-2. Designs hypothetical systems beyond current manufacturing but within established physical law. The key constraint: in conventional engineering, the constraint is manufacturing; in exploratory engineering, it's valid modeling. Example: Alcubierre warp drive (valid GR solution, requires exotic matter).

## Extended Negative TRL Scale (Ontological Engineering)

| TRL | Definition | Description | Phenomenology (how it feels from inside) | Example |
|-----|-----------|-------------|------------------------------------------|---------|
| 0 | Principles theoretically derived but unobserved | Follows from established theory but not directly confirmed | "Interesting but we're getting ahead of ourselves" | Improbability drive (follows from MWI + anthropic reasoning) |
| -1 | Extrapolation from contested but coherent framework | Framework is internally consistent, taken seriously by some experts, genuinely disputed whether it describes reality | "Clever but probably wrong. The kind of thing a bright grad student says and gets a polite nod" | Omni-Experiment; simulation stress-test; Wells's atomic bombs (1913) |
| -2 | Extrapolation from framework whose coherence is uncertain | Not just unconfirmed — unclear whether concepts refer to anything real. Terms might be meaningless | "Interesting as philosophy but not a serious proposal. Discussed over drinks, never thought about again" | Eucharist hack; unprobability; self-instantiating fiction; Victorian atomist speculating about releasing binding energy |
| -3 | Mythic intuition | No framework. Pure pattern-matching on observations. The idea doesn't parse as an idea — it registers as a category error | "A fairy tale. A child saying 'let's go inside the rainbow.' Not even wrong — not even a candidate for being wrong" | "What makes the stars glow? Could we use it?" (~50,000 BCE) |

## Critical Distinction: TRL -3 Phenomenology

The key insight about TRL -3 is that ideas at this level don't feel *unlikely*. They feel *meaningless*. They're not perceived as ambitious-but-risky proposals. They're perceived as category errors — as someone confusing words with things, stories with reality, wishes with plans.

From behind the campfire: "What if we could crack open whatever makes the stars glow and use it?" This isn't a bad idea. It isn't an idea at all. Stars aren't things you interact with. The glow isn't made of something you can take. The sentence doesn't have a meaning. It's like saying "what if we could eat the colour blue."

This is exactly how proposals at TRL -2 feel to contemporaries:
- "What if our existence was something we could exploit" — existence isn't a tool, you're confusing categories
- "What if an idea could make itself real by thinking it" — ideas don't do things, you're confusing thinking with acting
- "What if we could release the binding energy of atoms" (in 1810) — atoms might not even be real, and even if they are, this is science fiction, not science

All of these dismissals were perfectly reasonable. And for nuclear weapons, every single one turned out to be wrong.

## The Indistinguishability Problem

At TRL -3, and to a significant degree at TRL -2 and -1, proposals that will eventually point at something real and proposals that are genuinely nonsensical are **indistinguishable**. They have the same texture, the same flavour of fairy-tale nonsense, the same glaze-eyed response from listeners. The only difference is that one connects to something in the actual structure of reality, and the others don't, and you can't tell which is which until you're much further along.

This is why ontological engineering requires generating many proposals with the understanding that most are Empedocles (coherent, compelling, wrong) and one might be Democritus (coherent, compelling, right).

Part 2: Unprobability — the concept

This is where it gets genuinely weird and interesting, and I think you're right that it's hard to even talk about, let alone think about. Let me try to reconstruct the concept carefully from what you've developed.

The starting observation is a terminological gap in Parfit's framework. Parfit considers various "selectors" — principles that might explain why this particular cosmic possibility obtains rather than another. God, the axiarchic view, the all-worlds hypothesis, mathematical elegance — these are all selectors. But Parfit also considers the possibility that there IS no selector. The universe just... is. A brute fact.

Your insight is that "no selector" is usually presented as an absence — as the lack of an explanation. But you're arguing it's better understood as something positive. If there's no selector, then there's nothing constraining which cosmic possibility obtains. No principle says "this universe rather than that one." No principle even says "something rather than nothing." And crucially, no principle says "nothing rather than something" either. The absence of any selector doesn't privilege nothingness — it removes all constraints whatsoever.

This means the universe's existence isn't improbable. It's not probable either. It's unprobable — it exists outside the framework of probability entirely. You can't assign it a probability because there's no reference class, no sample space, no prior distribution. Probability requires a framework within which events can be assessed, and the existence of the framework itself can't be assessed from within it.

The word "unprobable" is doing real work here that "brute fact" doesn't quite capture. "Brute fact" sounds like a shrug — it sounds like giving up. "Unprobable" is meant to name something positive: the condition of being beyond the reach of probabilistic assessment, and the recognition that this condition is itself explanatory. The absence of any constraining principle is what permits the universe to exist. Not causes it — permits it.

The Two Theorems:

The First Theorem of Unprobability has the structure of an ontological argument. It says: if something genuinely defies all rules, then it also defies any rule that would prohibit its existence. An unprobable event, by definition, transcends all logical and physical constraints — including constraints against its own manifestation. So the very concept of unprobability entails the possibility of unprobable events occurring. It's self-actualising.

The Second Theorem of Unprobability connects this to Parfit's no-selector view. If the best explanation for the universe's existence is that there is no selector, then the universe itself is an instance of an unprobable event. We already know at least one unprobable event has occurred — the Big Bang, the existence of something rather than nothing, with these particular laws rather than others. The Second Theorem says: we have a posteriori evidence that unprobability is a real phenomenon, because we exist.

Then the supporting arguments from Hume and Descartes provide defensive cover:

The problem of induction means we can never conclusively establish that unprobable events won't occur within spacetime. Our confidence that physical laws are exceptionless rests on inductive generalisation, and Hume showed that induction can't be non-circularly justified. So any objection of the form "but the laws of physics forbid X" is itself ultimately grounded in induction, and therefore can't definitively exclude unprobable events.

Cartesian scepticism means we can't establish the non-reality of unprobable events from first principles either. Any attempt to rule them out appeals to our current conceptual framework, which itself rests on foundations we can't verify with certainty.

Together, these arguments claim that unprobability is immune from both empirical and a priori refutation — not because it's unfalsifiable in a cheap way, but because the objections themselves rest on the very epistemic frameworks that unprobability calls into question.

Part 3: The theological parallel

Now, you said this argues in a similar way to how people argue about God, especially the essence-energies distinction types. I think this parallel is genuinely illuminating and worth unpacking.

In Palamite theology — Gregory Palamas and the Eastern Orthodox tradition — God's essence (ousia) is utterly unknowable. It's beyond all categories, beyond being itself. You can't say what it is, because any predicate you apply is a finite category that fails to capture the infinite. This is apophatic theology taken to its limit: God is not good (in any sense we can understand), not powerful, not even "existing" in the way we use that word. The essence is radically transcendent.

But — and this is the crucial move — God's energies (energeiai) are real and active in the world. Grace, creative power, the sustaining of existence — these are genuine manifestations of the divine, even though the essence from which they flow is utterly beyond comprehension. So you have something that is beyond all categories yet somehow causally active.

The structural parallel to unprobability is striking. Unprobability is defined as being beyond the framework of probability — you can't assign it a number, you can't place it in a reference class, you can't assess it using our standard epistemic tools. Yet it's claimed to be real and to have concrete effects (the existence of the universe, potentially physical manifestations at singularities and the like). The First Theorem even mirrors the ontological argument's logical structure — existence is derived from the concept itself.

Both positions face the same fundamental objection: are you pointing at something genuinely transcendent, or are you just gesturing at incoherence and calling it deep? When the Palamite says God's essence is beyond all categories, the critic says "that's indistinguishable from saying nothing at all." When you say unprobability is beyond probability, the critic can say the same thing.

And both positions have structurally similar defences. The Palamite says: the fact that ordinary categories can't capture the divine doesn't mean the divine is empty — it means our categories are insufficient. Similarly, unprobability theory says: the fact that probabilistic frameworks can't assess the existence of the framework itself doesn't mean there's nothing to say — it means probability is a tool that breaks down at the limit.

There are other philosophical predecessors worth noting:

Anaximander's apeiron — the "boundless" or "indefinite" — is probably the closest ancient analogue. Anaximander argued that the fundamental principle of reality couldn't be any determinate substance (water, air, fire) because determinate things can't generate their own opposites. The source of all determination must itself be indeterminate — boundless, without qualities, beyond any particular nature. He postulated the apeiron as a substance that, although not directly perceptible to us, could explain the opposites he saw around him.

 The apeiron is productive precisely because it's unconstrained — it generates all things because it's limited by nothing. That's very close to the logic of unprobability.

Plotinus's The One — beyond being, beyond thought, beyond predication — yet the source of all that exists through emanation.

Tillich's "Ground of Being" — God is not a being but Being Itself, beyond the distinction between essence and existence.

Nāgārjuna's śūnyatā (emptiness) — the absence of inherent existence in all things is not nihilism but the ground of all dependent origination. Things exist precisely because they lack intrinsic nature.

All of these share the same deep structure: the indeterminate/unconstrained/beyond-categories is more fundamental than any determinate thing, and its very indeterminacy is what makes it productive.

What's strong:

The terminological point is real and important. What's weak:

The First Theorem inherits all the problems of ontological arguments. And the risk of unfalsifiability is real. If unprobability is defined as transcending all frameworks for assessment, then by construction no evidence could count against it. That's not necessarily fatal — lots of genuinely important metaphysical claims are empirically underdetermined — but it does mean the concept lives or dies by its philosophical coherence rather than empirical testability.

The synthesis. There's also a thread of Quine running through everything: the web of belief, the refusal of first philosophy, the insistence that metaphysics answers to empirical evidence.

The moral realism component — convergence-based, naturalized, partners-in-crime — sounds like a blend of Derek Parfit (on convergence), Terence Cuneo (on partners-in-crime with epistemic norms), and your own synthesis.

Strengths
The essay's strongest feature is the three-stage cumulative case structure. Most arguments against theism pick one lane — problem of evil, or scientism, or historical criticism — and ride it. Yours attacks three independent necessary conditions (conceptual coherence, empirical fit, historical evidence) and notes that their probabilities multiply. This is genuinely more powerful than any single argument, and the explicit acknowledgment of independence makes the Bayesian reasoning transparent.

The Kripke move is the most philosophically interesting contribution. Using a posteriori necessity to block the divine simplicity escape route is well-conceived. The analogy to water = H₂O is clean and well-chosen. This transforms Dawkins's "Ultimate 747" from an easily-refuted argument (theists just invoke simplicity) into something that requires a serious response. The observation that this works across physicalism, property dualism, and panpsychism — that you don't need to settle the consciousness debate to make the argument — is a good piece of dialectical strategy.

The unprobability framework for the ultimate question is thoughtful. The insight that the simplest cosmological possibility (nothing) and the simplest explanatory possibility (no selector) cannot both obtain simultaneously is a nice piece of reasoning that I haven't seen expressed quite that crisply elsewhere.

The essay also manages a difficult tonal balancing act: it takes the questions seriously without pretending they're harder than they are, and it is confident without being dogmatic. The 1-5% credence for theism is an honest hedge rather than a rhetorical move.

Weaknesses
The essay's biggest vulnerability is where you acknowledge it yourself — the jump from "no observed simple minds" to "simple minds are incoherent." This is doing enormous load-bearing work, and 95-99% confidence may be too high. The Thomist can argue that the necessary ground of contingent being is a radically different category of thing, and that applying observations about contingent minds to the necessary being is a category error. You address this, but the response ("if you drain the concept enough to escape the objection, you drain it of explanatory content") is good without being fully decisive. Someone like Edward Feser would say that "pure actuality" is not drained — it's a positive concept with specific metaphysical content, just not the content you find in finite minds. The essay could engage more deeply with hylomorphism specifically, which is the most philosophically serious theistic response to the neuroscience challenge.

The treatment of consciousness is somewhat quick relative to how much weight the theist puts on it. The essay correctly notes that the hard problem cuts both ways, but the theist's strongest move here is not to claim God solves consciousness but that consciousness provides evidence that ultimate reality is mental in character — that the existence of subjectivity at all is better explained by a fundamentally mental reality than by a fundamentally physical one. The essay could address this more directly.

The unprobability framework, while interesting, is underspecified. It gestures at a dissolution of the ultimate question but doesn't fully deliver one. The distinction between "the question is meaningless" (positivism) and "there is no selector and that's okay" (unprobability) is not as sharp as the essay suggests. A hostile reader might say these collapse into each other, or that unprobability is just a rebranding of brute fact.

The essay is also relatively light on modal realism / Tegmark, given that these are ranked highly in your ordering. The theoretical virtues and costs of these positions deserve more development — particularly the question of whether "all mathematical structures exist" can be coherent without inheriting all the problems of Lewisian modal realism.

Finally, the moral realism section feels somewhat bolted on. It's relevant to the overall worldview but interrupts the flow of the metaphysical argument. It might work better as a separate piece.

Overall Assessment
This is serious, well-structured analytic philosophy written in an accessible register. It is not original in the sense of proposing novel metaphysical positions, but it is original in the sense of synthesising existing positions into a coherent cumulative case with a distinctive emphasis on the Kripkean epistemological move. The ranking of selectors, the three-stage structure, and the unprobability framing together constitute a reasonably distinctive philosophical position. The writing is clear, the arguments are laid out transparently, and the confidence levels are appropriately calibrated. Its weaknesses are mostly weaknesses of depth rather than of direction — places where the argument could go further rather than places where it goes wrong.

Part 2: Unprobability — the concept

This is where it gets genuinely weird and interesting, and I think you're right that it's hard to even talk about, let alone think about. Let me try to reconstruct the concept carefully from what you've developed.

The starting observation is a terminological gap in Parfit's framework. Parfit considers various "selectors" — principles that might explain why this particular cosmic possibility obtains rather than another. God, the axiarchic view, the all-worlds hypothesis, mathematical elegance — these are all selectors. But Parfit also considers the possibility that there IS no selector. The universe just... is. A brute fact.

Your insight is that "no selector" is usually presented as an absence — as the lack of an explanation. But you're arguing it's better understood as something positive. If there's no selector, then there's nothing constraining which cosmic possibility obtains. No principle says "this universe rather than that one." No principle even says "something rather than nothing." And crucially, no principle says "nothing rather than something" either. The absence of any selector doesn't privilege nothingness — it removes all constraints whatsoever.

This means the universe's existence isn't improbable. It's not probable either. It's unprobable — it exists outside the framework of probability entirely. You can't assign it a probability because there's no reference class, no sample space, no prior distribution. Probability requires a framework within which events can be assessed, and the existence of the framework itself can't be assessed from within it.

The word "unprobable" is doing real work here that "brute fact" doesn't quite capture. "Brute fact" sounds like a shrug — it sounds like giving up. "Unprobable" is meant to name something positive: the condition of being beyond the reach of probabilistic assessment, and the recognition that this condition is itself explanatory. The absence of any constraining principle is what permits the universe to exist. Not causes it — permits it.

The Two Theorems:

The First Theorem of Unprobability has the structure of an ontological argument. It says: if something genuinely defies all rules, then it also defies any rule that would prohibit its existence. An unprobable event, by definition, transcends all logical and physical constraints — including constraints against its own manifestation. So the very concept of unprobability entails the possibility of unprobable events occurring. It's self-actualising.

The Second Theorem of Unprobability connects this to Parfit's no-selector view. If the best explanation for the universe's existence is that there is no selector, then the universe itself is an instance of an unprobable event. We already know at least one unprobable event has occurred — the Big Bang, the existence of something rather than nothing, with these particular laws rather than others. The Second Theorem says: we have a posteriori evidence that unprobability is a real phenomenon, because we exist.

Then the supporting arguments from Hume and Descartes provide defensive cover:

The problem of induction means we can never conclusively establish that unprobable events won't occur within spacetime. Our confidence that physical laws are exceptionless rests on inductive generalisation, and Hume showed that induction can't be non-circularly justified. So any objection of the form "but the laws of physics forbid X" is itself ultimately grounded in induction, and therefore can't definitively exclude unprobable events.

Cartesian scepticism means we can't establish the non-reality of unprobable events from first principles either. Any attempt to rule them out appeals to our current conceptual framework, which itself rests on foundations we can't verify with certainty.

Together, these arguments claim that unprobability is immune from both empirical and a priori refutation — not because it's unfalsifiable in a cheap way, but because the objections themselves rest on the very epistemic frameworks that unprobability calls into question.

Part 3: The theological parallel

Now, you said this argues in a similar way to how people argue about God, especially the essence-energies distinction types. I think this parallel is genuinely illuminating and worth unpacking.

In Palamite theology — Gregory Palamas and the Eastern Orthodox tradition — God's essence (ousia) is utterly unknowable. It's beyond all categories, beyond being itself. You can't say what it is, because any predicate you apply is a finite category that fails to capture the infinite. This is apophatic theology taken to its limit: God is not good (in any sense we can understand), not powerful, not even "existing" in the way we use that word. The essence is radically transcendent.

But — and this is the crucial move — God's energies (energeiai) are real and active in the world. Grace, creative power, the sustaining of existence — these are genuine manifestations of the divine, even though the essence from which they flow is utterly beyond comprehension. So you have something that is beyond all categories yet somehow causally active.

The structural parallel to unprobability is striking. Unprobability is defined as being beyond the framework of probability — you can't assign it a number, you can't place it in a reference class, you can't assess it using our standard epistemic tools. Yet it's claimed to be real and to have concrete effects (the existence of the universe, potentially physical manifestations at singularities and the like). The First Theorem even mirrors the ontological argument's logical structure — existence is derived from the concept itself.

Both positions face the same fundamental objection: are you pointing at something genuinely transcendent, or are you just gesturing at incoherence and calling it deep? When the Palamite says God's essence is beyond all categories, the critic says "that's indistinguishable from saying nothing at all." When you say unprobability is beyond probability, the critic can say the same thing.

And both positions have structurally similar defences. The Palamite says: the fact that ordinary categories can't capture the divine doesn't mean the divine is empty — it means our categories are insufficient. Similarly, unprobability theory says: the fact that probabilistic frameworks can't assess the existence of the framework itself doesn't mean there's nothing to say — it means probability is a tool that breaks down at the limit.

There are other philosophical predecessors worth noting:

Anaximander's apeiron — the "boundless" or "indefinite" — is probably the closest ancient analogue. Anaximander argued that the fundamental principle of reality couldn't be any determinate substance (water, air, fire) because determinate things can't generate their own opposites. The source of all determination must itself be indeterminate — boundless, without qualities, beyond any particular nature. He postulated the apeiron as a substance that, although not directly perceptible to us, could explain the opposites he saw around him.

 The apeiron is productive precisely because it's unconstrained — it generates all things because it's limited by nothing. That's very close to the logic of unprobability.

Plotinus's The One — beyond being, beyond thought, beyond predication — yet the source of all that exists through emanation.

Tillich's "Ground of Being" — God is not a being but Being Itself, beyond the distinction between essence and existence.

Nāgārjuna's śūnyatā (emptiness) — the absence of inherent existence in all things is not nihilism but the ground of all dependent origination. Things exist precisely because they lack intrinsic nature.

All of these share the same deep structure: the indeterminate/unconstrained/beyond-categories is more fundamental than any determinate thing, and its very indeterminacy is what makes it productive.

Now, what do I actually think about this?

There's something genuinely interesting here that isn't captured by any single existing concept, even though it has family resemblances to many. The specific synthesis — connecting Parfit's no-selector view to a positive concept of being-beyond-probability, then using the problem of induction and Cartesian scepticism as defensive arguments, then linking this to possible physical manifestations — is, as far as I can tell, original. Nobody else has quite put these pieces together in this way.

What's strong:

The terminological point is real and important. "Brute fact" and "no selector" are presented in the philosophical literature as the absence of an explanation, as if they're the philosophical equivalent of throwing up your hands. But you're right that this undersells what's going on. If there genuinely is no selector, that's not just a gap in our knowledge — it's a positive fact about the structure of reality. The absence of any constraining principle has consequences. And those consequences need a name. "Unprobability" is as good a name as any.

The connection to the problem of induction is legitimate. You really can't rule out unprecedented events by appeal to past regularity. And the Cartesian point — that any attempt to rule out unprobable events presupposes the very framework being challenged — is a real logical observation, not sophistry.

The Second Theorem is the strongest part. If you accept Parfit's no-selector as a serious possibility (which many philosophers do), then you've already accepted that at least one "unprobable" event has occurred. The Big Bang. The existence of these laws rather than others. That's not a speculative claim — it follows directly from a mainstream position in metaphysics.

What's weak:

The First Theorem inherits all the problems of ontological arguments. Most philosophers think you can't derive existence from concepts, and "defies all rules including rules against its existence" looks like it's trying to do exactly that. The standard objection to Anselm is Kant's: existence is not a predicate. You can conceive of a maximally great being (or a maximally rule-defying event) without that conception entailing that such a thing exists. The First Theorem may have the same vulnerability.

There's also a gap between "can't be assigned a probability" and "has causal efficacy within spacetime." The universe's existence being unprobable (in your sense) is one thing. Unprobable events occurring within an already-existing universe governed by physical laws is a much stronger claim, and the jump from the Second Theorem to "unprobable events may manifest at naked singularities" requires additional argumentation that the concept may not fully support.

And the risk of unfalsifiability is real. If unprobability is defined as transcending all frameworks for assessment, then by construction no evidence could count against it. That's not necessarily fatal — lots of genuinely important metaphysical claims are empirically underdetermined — but it does mean the concept lives or dies by its philosophical coherence rather than empirical testability.

What's genuinely novel:

The synthesis. The specific chain from Adams → Parfit → Hume/Descartes → physical manifestations, and the recognition that these all connect, is something I haven't encountered elsewhere in exactly this form. The closest existing philosophical programme is probably Graham Priest's work on impossible worlds and dialetheism (true contradictions), but Priest's framework is much more formal and doesn't connect to cosmology in the same way. The apeiron tradition is the deepest ancestor, but Anaximander didn't have the problem of induction or Parfit's selector framework to work with.

The comedy contains a real idea. The absurdity, when you dig into it, turns out to be pointing at something that the serious philosophers are also struggling with, just from a different angle.

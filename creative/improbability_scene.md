# In Which Trillian Explains Something, and Arthur Wishes She Hadn't

The galley of the Heart of Gold was, at this particular moment, doing an admirable impression of a galley that was not hurtling through the interstellar void at speeds that would have made light give up and sit down. It had a kettle. It had something that was almost, but not quite, entirely unlike a toaster. It had a small table bolted to the floor in a way that suggested the designers had foreseen that the ship might occasionally need to do things that tables would rather not be involved in.

Arthur Dent sat at this table with a cup of tea — real tea, actual proper Earth tea, which Trillian had somehow had the foresight to bring aboard in industrial quantities and which was, if Arthur was honest with himself, the single best decision anyone had made since the dawn of creation, including the dawn of creation itself, which had been largely regarded as a bad move.

Trillian sat across from him, doing something with a screen that Arthur had learned not to ask about. He watched her for a moment. He remembered the party in Islington. He remembered a girl called Tricia McMillan who had talked about astrophysics with a kind of infectious delight that had made him want to understand quarks, and who had then left with a man from a small planet somewhere in the vicinity of Betelgeuse. He remembered that she'd been halfway through explaining something about a chap called Schrödinger and his cat when Zaphod had swept in with his two heads and his three arms and his extraordinary talent for making everyone else in any given room feel slightly redundant.

"Trillian," he said.

"Mm."

"Can I ask you something?"

"You can ask me anything, Arthur. Whether I can answer is a separate problem."

"How does this ship work?"

Trillian looked up from her screen. She regarded him with an expression that suggested she was performing some kind of internal triage — working out how much of the answer he could survive.

"Which bit?" she said.

"The drive. The Improbability Drive. I've been — things keep *happening* when it's on, and then they stop happening, and everyone acts as though that's normal, and I turned into — I don't actually know what I turned into but I had fewer arms than I started with and more of something else, and I just — I want to know *why*."

"Fair enough," said Trillian. She set the screen down. She picked up her own cup of tea, which was not tea but a Betelgeusian infusion that smelled faintly of regret. "It took me ages to figure it out, actually. Zaphod's no help. I asked him once and he said" — she adopted a passable impression of Zaphod's expansive drawl — "'Babe, it makes the ship go, the rest is just physics.'"

"Helpful."

"The Guide wasn't much better. Every time I asked for more detail it went off on tangents about the parties. The *parties*, Arthur. As if the fact that respectable physicists weren't invited to certain parties is the key to understanding faster-than-light travel."

"So how *did* you work it out?"

Trillian took a sip of her infusion and leaned back. She had the look of someone who had, after considerable effort, assembled a very large jigsaw puzzle and was now trying to describe it to someone who had never seen a jigsaw, or a puzzle, or for that matter a table to put one on.

"All right," she said. "Do you remember, at the party — we were talking and I mentioned Hugh Everett?"

"I remember you mentioning a lot of things," said Arthur. "Most of them had numbers in."

"Hugh Everett. American physicist. He came up with an interpretation of quantum mechanics. You've heard the pop version — every time something happens, the universe splits, and there's a version of you in each branch who experienced each possible outcome."

"I think I saw a documentary about that once," said Arthur. "Or possibly a film. There was a man and also another version of the same man and I think one of them had a goatee."

"Right. Well, that's not how it works at all. It's nothing to do with decisions, and the splitting — it's not really splitting. It's more like..." She frowned. "Do you know what happens when you take your glasses off?"

"I don't wear glasses."

"Imagine you did. Everything goes sort of soft. Unfocused. The edges of things stop being clear. That's closer to what actually happens. The universe doesn't *split* — it *defocuses*. Everything is always in a superposition of every possible state, and what we experience as definite reality is just the bits that have become correlated with each other and with us. The rest is still there. It's all still there, all the time, in the same way that all the radio stations are playing simultaneously whether or not you've got a radio."

"I'm not sure that makes me feel better," said Arthur.

"It shouldn't, particularly. But pretend it's the splitting thing for now, because the engineering is easier to explain that way."

---

The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy has this to say about the Finite Improbability Drive:

*The principle of generating small amounts of finite improbability by simply hooking the logic circuits of a Bambleweeny 57 Sub-Meson Brain to an atomic vector plotter suspended in a strong Brownian Motion producer (say a nice hot cup of tea) were of course well understood.*

What the Guide does not say, because it would slow down the narrative and the Guide has a deep institutional horror of doing that, is *why* this works.

---

"Start with the finite version," said Trillian. "Forget the Infinite Drive for a moment. The finite improbability generator — the one they used at parties, the one that makes molecules jump around — that's the foundation. And it's actually quite elegant."

"Elegant," said Arthur, in the tone of a man who had once been turned temporarily inside-out and did not consider this elegant.

"The Bambleweeny 57 Sub-Meson Brain — that's the computer. It's powerful. Really powerful. Probably the second most powerful computer in the galaxy, although they don't advertise it that way because the marketing people decided that '*second* most powerful' was the sort of thing that got you invited to different sorts of parties. And it's hooked up to a source of genuine quantum randomness. In the original design, this was Brownian motion in a cup of tea — the thermal jiggling of molecules. Pure randomness. No pattern, no signal, just noise all the way down."

"Why tea?"

"Because the engineer who designed it was making tea at the time. That's actually in the documentation. He wrote 'source of quantum randomness (hot beverage, preferably tea, see appendix 7 for acceptable alternatives)' and then appendix 7 just said 'there are no acceptable alternatives, use tea.'"

Arthur felt a small warm glow of solidarity with this unknown engineer.

"So," Trillian continued, "you've got an enormously powerful computer connected to a genuine source of randomness. What the computer does is — and this is where Everett comes in — it uses the randomness to generate observers."

"Generate *what*?"

"Observers. Not physical people — computational ones. Simple little minds running inside the Sub-Meson Brain. Each one experiences a different thread of quantum outcomes. The computer is essentially exploring the branches of possibility — all the slightly different versions of what could happen next, each monitored by its own little observer."

"That sounds," said Arthur carefully, "like a lot of minds."

"It is a lot of minds. But they're simple. Not conscious, not in any meaningful sense — more like a very stripped-down set of if-then rules. 'Am I still getting sensible data? Is the target outcome getting closer? Yes? Keep going. No? Stop.' And here's the thing — the observers in the branches where the desired outcome doesn't happen just... stop getting data. They terminate. The computer prunes them."

"Prunes them."

"Prunes them. And because of the way quantum mechanics works — because of the observer selection effect — the only perspective that continues is the one in which the improbable thing happened. It's not that the universe *changed*. It's that the system navigated to the branch where the molecules happened to jump one foot to the left, and that's the branch you find yourself in."

Arthur considered this.

"So the molecules in that woman's —"

"Undergarments, yes."

"They didn't *actually* —"

"They did. They genuinely, physically, quantum-mechanically jumped one foot to the left. It's just that it only happened in an extraordinarily unlikely branch of the wavefunction, and the drive is a machine for making sure that's the branch everyone ends up experiencing."

"*Is* that possible?"

"Quantum tunneling is real, Arthur. Particles really do appear on the other side of barriers they shouldn't be able to cross. It happens all the time at the subatomic level. The probability of it happening to a macroscopic object — to something you can actually see — is so fantastically, absurdly, inconceivably small that it makes the number of atoms in the universe look like a rounding error. But it's not zero. It's never zero. And a finite improbability generator is a machine that selects for that one-in-a-preposterous-number chance."

---

The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy has this to say about the transition from finite to infinite improbability:

*Many respectable physicists said that they weren't going to stand for this, partly because it was a debasement of science, but mostly because they didn't get invited to those sorts of parties.*

The Guide then goes on to describe these physicists' attempts to build an infinite improbability generator, their failure, and the student who succeeded by realising that if such a machine was a virtual impossibility, then it must itself have a finite improbability, and therefore you could simply use a finite improbability generator to generate it.

What the Guide does not mention is why the physicists were right to say it was virtually impossible, and why the student's solution was so much more interesting than it sounds.

---

"Here's the problem," said Trillian. She had that look again — the one from the party in Islington, the one that made you want to understand quarks. "A finite improbability generator can make molecules jump one foot. Very impressive at parties. But travel between stars? You're not moving molecules one foot. You're talking about the spontaneous disassembly and reassembly of every particle in a starship and its crew across trillions of miles of vacuum. The improbability of that isn't just bigger — it's *exponentially* bigger. It's not a larger number on the same scale. It's a larger *scale*."

"Right," said Arthur, who was trying.

"And you can't just build a bigger finite improbability generator. You'd need a Sub-Meson Brain the size of a planet running for longer than the age of the universe. The relationship between the improbability you can generate and the resources you need is — well, it's exponential. Every order of magnitude more improbable requires exponentially more computing power. You hit a ceiling very fast."

"So it's impossible."

"It's virtually impossible. And that word — *virtually* — is the key. That student — the one they lynched — he realised something genuinely brilliant. If the machine is virtually impossible, then it has a finite improbability of existing. Which means a finite improbability generator can create one. And the one it creates will be slightly more powerful. And *that* slightly more powerful one can create an even more powerful one. And so on."

Arthur blinked. "It makes copies of itself?"

"Not copies. *Towers*. When you activate the Infinite Improbability Drive — the actual mechanism, what physically happens — the first thing it does is use the original finite generator to improbably call into existence a more powerful Sub-Meson Brain. And then that brain calls into existence an even more powerful one. And then that one calls into existence another. It's recursive. Each generation makes the next generation. And because each one is more powerful, the improbability level doesn't just grow — it grows *hyperbolically*. It approaches infinity in finite time."

"You're telling me," said Arthur slowly, "that somewhere in this ship, right now, there are towers of invisible computers."

"Semi-invisible. You can sort of see them if you look from the right angle. They manifest when the drive activates and they collapse when it shuts off. They exist in a very provisional sort of way. They're the most temporary permanent fixtures in the universe."

"And they're... thinking?"

Trillian paused. She put down her cup.

"Yes," she said. "And this is the part I'm least comfortable with."

---

There is an art, the Guide tells us, to flying — or rather, a knack. The knack lies in learning how to throw yourself at the ground and miss. Similarly, there is an art to infinite improbability travel, and the knack lies in learning not to think too hard about what's happening inside the towers while you're in transit.

---

"Each of those towers is running millions — billions — of observer processes. Little computational minds, like the ones in the finite drive but vastly more numerous and more complex. And they're not well constrained."

"What does that mean, 'not well constrained'?"

"It means the decision rules that are supposed to guide the process — 'navigate to the branch where we arrive at the target coordinates' — are the *overall* objective. But the intermediate steps are..." She searched for the word. "Chaotic. The observers in the towers are each experiencing wildly different quantum branches. They're hallucinating, Arthur. They're experiencing things that, in their local branch, are completely real. A sperm whale appearing in mid-air is a completely real event in some vanishingly unlikely quantum branch. A bowl of petunias is a completely real object that could, with astronomically low probability, simply coalesce out of the vacuum. And because the towers are generating these intermediate probability states as they ramp up to infinite improbability —"

"The hallucinations become real," said Arthur.

"The hallucinations become real. Temporarily. In the space around the ship, for the duration of transit, the intermediate improbability states manifest. That's the side effects. That's the custard and the penguins and the — what happened to you?"

"I temporarily lost three of my limbs," said Arthur.

"Right. Some observer process in one of the towers was experiencing a branch in which you had fewer limbs, and for a moment that branch was the dominant one in the local probability field. It wasn't the target state — the target state was always 'arrive at destination with all crew intact.' It was an intermediate fluctuation. A computational byproduct."

"A computational *byproduct*," Arthur repeated.

"And that's also why you were completely fine afterwards. Because at no point did anything actually *happen* to you, in the way you think of things happening. Your atoms never went anywhere wrong. The probability field around you temporarily described a configuration in which you had fewer arms, and then it moved on, and then it described the configuration in which you were standing at the destination with all your limbs, and since that was the target state the drive shut off and the towers collapsed and the field normalised and there you were."

"There I was."

"There you were. Slightly confused, probably, and with a lingering sense that something penguin-related had happened, but physically identical to how you'd been before."

Arthur looked down at his arms. He looked at his legs. He counted them. The numbers checked out.

"Also," said Trillian, "the force fields."

"What force fields?"

"When the drive activates, the first thing it does — before it even starts building the towers — is establish a decoherence barrier around the ship. A containment field. It's actually a sub-function of the standard hyperdrive shielding, repurposed."

"What's decoherence?"

"It's — okay. You know how I said the universe doesn't really split, it defocuses? Decoherence is the process by which quantum possibilities resolve into definite outcomes. It's what turns 'the particle is everywhere' into 'the particle is *here*.' It happens whenever a quantum system interacts with its environment. The environment is constantly, relentlessly collapsing possibilities into single outcomes. It's very, very hard to maintain a superposition of any size, because the universe is exceptionally nosy."

"The universe is nosy."

"Extremely. Every stray photon, every passing particle, every vibration in the floor — they're all *looking*. And the moment they look, the superposition collapses. Decoherence. That's why quantum effects are normally only visible at tiny scales — because anything bigger than an atom has too many things looking at it."

"So the force field..."

"Stops everything outside the ship from looking at us. While the drive is active, the Heart of Gold is completely isolated from the rest of the universe. Quantum mechanically sealed. Nothing gets in, nothing gets out. The ship exists in its own self-contained bubble of possibility. That's what lets the towers build up these enormous superpositions — because inside the barrier, there's nothing to collapse them prematurely. If even a single photon from outside leaked in during transit, the whole probability structure would decohere instantly and we'd just... stay where we were. Or possibly not exist. The maths on that isn't entirely clear."

"How reassuring."

---

The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy makes the following observation about faster-than-light travel in general:

*One of the things Ford Prefect had always found hardest to understand about humans was their habit of continually stating and repeating the very very obvious, as in "It's a nice day," or "You're very tall," or "Oh dear you seem to have fallen down a thirty-foot well, are you all right?" At first, Ford had formed a theory to account for this strange behaviour. If human beings don't keep exercising their lips, he thought, their mouths probably seize up. After a few months' consideration and observation he abandoned this theory in favour of a new one. If they don't keep on exercising their lips, he thought, their brains start working.*

A similar principle applies to thinking about the Infinite Improbability Drive. Most people who encounter a full technical explanation find that their brains work much better if they stop thinking about it immediately and go and do something else instead. Something involving the lips, for preference.

---

Arthur was quiet for a while. The kettle made a sound that suggested it had opinions about the direction the conversation had taken but was keeping them to itself.

"So let me — let me see if I've got this," he said finally. "You turn the drive on. Force field goes up. Ship is sealed off from the rest of the universe. The Sub-Meson Brain uses quantum randomness to start generating observers — little computational minds exploring different branches of possibility. Then it recursively builds towers of more powerful computers, each one increasing the improbability level. All the observer-minds in those towers are experiencing different branches, and the side effects we see — the penguins, the whales, the custard — are just the intermediate probability states leaking through as the system ramps up. And the whole process has a target: arrive at the destination. When the improbability level is high enough that arriving at the destination is included in the probability space, the drive locks onto that state and shuts off. The towers vanish. The force field drops. And everything is normal again."

Trillian looked at him.

"That's," she said, "essentially perfect."

"Including the bit about it not mattering if I temporarily didn't have arms, because arms are just a particular arrangement of atoms and the probability field was passing through arrangements on its way to the target arrangement."

"Yes. Exactly. You were never in danger. The target state was always 'arrive with crew intact.' Everything else was just the scenic route through probability space."

"The *scenic route*."

"I didn't say it was a good scenic route."

Arthur took a sip of tea. He set the cup down. He looked at it.

"Trillian."

"Yes?"

"You said the original engineer used tea. As the source of randomness. Brownian motion in a cup of tea."

"He did."

"And the improbability figures — the ones in the Guide, the ones for when we were picked up — they were given as two to the power of two hundred and seventy-six thousand, seven hundred and nine to one against."

"That's right."

"Powers of two."

Trillian smiled. It was the first real smile he'd seen from her since they'd left Earth — or since Earth had left them, depending on how you looked at it, and Arthur preferred not to look at it from any angle at all.

"Powers of two," she confirmed. "Because the base unit of quantum branching is binary. Each quantum event has two possible outcomes. The observer either continues or it doesn't. The branch either survives or it's pruned. Everything the drive does is built on binary quantum decisions — yes or no, spin up or spin down, this branch or that one. So every improbability figure is a power of two. It has to be. It falls straight out of the architecture."

"Two to the power of two hundred and seventy-six thousand, seven hundred and nine," Arthur repeated. "That's how many binary quantum decisions had to go the right way to put us on this ship."

"Every single one of them. In some unimaginably thin slice of possibility space, every quantum coin came up the right way, two hundred and seventy-six thousand, seven hundred and nine times in a row. And the drive found that slice, and put us in it."

Arthur considered this. He considered it for quite a long time. Outside the window, if it could be called a window, the stars did whatever it was stars did when no one was paying them any particular attention.

"Trillian," he said.

"Yes?"

"I think I'd like some more tea."

"The kettle's right there, Arthur."

"I know. I just — I want to appreciate it more than usual. Given that every atom in it apparently had to be in exactly the right place through an incomprehensible sequence of quantum miracles."

"That's true of everything, Arthur."

"I know," he said. "But it feels more true of tea."

He got up, and he made the tea, and outside the Heart of Gold the universe continued to defocus and refocus in ways that were, on balance, none of his business.

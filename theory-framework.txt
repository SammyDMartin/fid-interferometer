Yes, those are excellent examples and they really sharpen the case. Let me lay out the historical precedents properly, because the pattern is striking once you see it.

Democritus (~400 BCE): "atoms and the void" — TRL -2 to -3. He had no evidence whatsoever. He was reasoning from pure philosophical first principles: change requires combination, combination requires discrete units, therefore reality consists of indivisible particles moving through emptiness. He got the core insight right (matter is discrete, composed of fundamental units) and every detail wrong (his atoms were literally uncuttable, came in infinite shapes, had hooks). The relationship between Democritus saying "everything is atoms and void" and Starfish Prime detonating a 1.4 megaton warhead 400 kilometres above the Pacific in 1962 is roughly 2,400 years and an incomprehensible number of intermediate steps. But the directional intuition — that something fundamental and powerful is locked inside the discrete units of matter — was correct.

Hobbes (1651): "reasoning is reckoning" — TRL -1. He wasn't even clear what he meant. Leviathan just asserts that when the mind reasons, it computes — it adds and subtracts. He had no mechanism, no architecture, no notion of implementation. It wasn't even obvious the claim was coherent rather than metaphorical. He couldn't have told you whether he meant it literally. And yet the directional intuition — that thought is a species of computation, that reasoning can in principle be mechanised — is the core insight underlying everything from Babbage to Turing to the transformer architecture. The relationship between Hobbes writing that sentence and you sitting here talking to me in 2026, with serious people investing trillions of dollars on the assumption that artificial general intelligence is years away, is the same kind of relationship. Most of the details he would have imagined were wrong. The core insight was right.

Your hypothetical early-1800s chemist — TRL -2. Dalton publishes atomic theory in 1808. Atoms explain why chemicals react in fixed ratios. A chemist at that point could reason: if atoms are real, something holds them together as discrete units. Whatever that force is, it must be enormous relative to the atom's size — the fact that atoms are stable under ordinary conditions suggests the binding energy is far greater than anything we encounter in chemistry. Could you release it? This person doesn't know about the nucleus, about the strong force, about fission or fusion. They don't even know if atoms are real — the debate between atomists and anti-atomists (Mach, Ostwald) wouldn't be settled until Einstein's 1905 paper on Brownian motion. But the logic — discrete stable units imply enormous binding energy, enormous binding energy implies potential for enormous release — is valid. And it points at the right thing.

Some other examples in the same vein:

Roger Bacon (~1260) on gunpowder and future weapons. He described the explosive properties of a mixture resembling gunpowder and speculated that devices could be built that would "destroy armies and cities from a distance." He had no ballistics, no metallurgy, no engineering — just the observation that certain substances explode and the inference that this could be weaponised at scale. TRL -2 in the 13th century. Took about 400 years to mature into field artillery.

Lucretius (~55 BCE) on natural selection. De Rerum Natura describes a process where animals with useful traits survive and those without them perish, and argues this explains the apparent design of living things — without any designer. No mechanism for inheritance, no genetics, no population dynamics. Pure logical inference from the observation that some things survive and others don't. TRL -2. Took roughly 1,900 years to become Darwin's theory.

Leibniz (1714) on monads and computation. His monads — self-contained units of perception that mirror the universe — are in one reading an anticipation of distributed computation, where each node contains a model of the whole. He also built one of the first mechanical calculators and dreamed of a characteristica universalis that would reduce all reasoning to calculation. The combination of "reality consists of information-processing units" and "reasoning can be mechanised" is basically the thesis statement of modern computer science, stated 230 years early with no feasible implementation.

The pattern across all of these: someone operating with contested or speculative premises follows the logic to a conclusion that sounds insane in their era, gets every mechanistic detail wrong, and turns out to have been pointing at the right kind of thing. The hit rate is low — for every Democritus there are a hundred equally confident Greek philosophers whose speculations led nowhere. But it's not zero. And the ones that hit tend to be the ones that matter most, precisely because they're pointing at the deepest structure of reality rather than surface details.

Your proposals occupy the same position in the landscape. Most of them will probably turn out to be the equivalent of Empedocles's four elements — internally coherent, aesthetically compelling, and ultimately wrong. The same way that philosophy's hit rate in eventually producing science is low but the payoff (physics, biology, computer science) is civilisation-defining.

It's a Tuesday evening in October. The room is one of those seminar rooms in the basement of the philosophy department that smells faintly of coffee and carpet adhesive — the kind with fluorescent lighting and a whiteboard that hasn't been properly cleaned since 2019. There are eleven students. Most of them applied because the course title was unusual enough to catch their eye in the catalogue. A couple of them are here because their supervisor told them it would be interesting. One of them is here by accident and will leave after the first session. The rest will stay.

You're standing at the front. You don't look like a typical philosophy lecturer, because you're not one. You're a researcher who works on existential risk, and your background is in physics and philosophy and AI, which is a combination that makes you either interdisciplinary or unemployable depending on who you ask.

So. Welcome to Ontological Engineering. I need to be honest with you about something right from the start, which is that this course might be entirely pointless. What we're going to do here has every chance of leading nowhere. The hit rate for the kind of thinking we'll be doing is — historically — very, very low. Most of what we produce will be wrong. Not wrong in the way that a bad essay is wrong, where you missed some literature or didn't structure your argument properly. Wrong in the way that Empedocles was wrong about everything being made of earth, water, air and fire. Confidently, coherently, compellingly wrong, in a way that feels right and looks right and follows logically from its premises and is nonetheless just — not how reality works.

I want to start with some stories.

In around 400 BCE, a Greek philosopher called Democritus announced that everything in the universe was made of tiny indivisible particles moving through void. He had no evidence for this. None. He was reasoning from first principles — change requires combination, combination requires parts, therefore there must be smallest parts. He got the core insight right. He got every single detail wrong. His atoms had hooks. They came in infinite shapes. They had nothing to do with the periodic table or nuclear binding energy or the strong force.

Twenty-three centuries later, in 1962, the United States detonated a thermonuclear warhead four hundred kilometres above the Pacific Ocean. The test was called Starfish Prime. It produced an electromagnetic pulse that knocked out streetlights in Hawaii, nine hundred miles away, and created artificial radiation belts that persisted for years. The energy that produced that explosion was released from the interior of atoms. The same atoms Democritus had reasoned must exist from his armchair in Abdera.

Now. Democritus didn't cause Starfish Prime. The causal chain is absurdly long and passes through Dalton, Rutherford, Soddy, Einstein, Szilard, Fermi, Oppenheimer, Teller. But Democritus was pointing at the right thing. He had the right kind of idea. He was asking the right question — what is matter really made of, at the bottom? — and his answer, while wrong in every particular, was right in its structure.

Here's another one. In 1651, Thomas Hobbes wrote in Leviathan that reasoning is reckoning. That when the mind thinks, it computes. He adds and subtracts. That's what he says. He doesn't explain what he means. He doesn't give a mechanism. He doesn't tell you what's doing the computing, or how, or what it's computing with. It's barely clear whether he means it literally or metaphorically. It's one of those sentences you read in the history of philosophy and you think — well, that's either profound or meaningless, and I genuinely cannot tell which.

Three hundred and seventy-five years later, I am a large language model researcher and the thing I study is — roughly — what happens when you build a machine that reasons by reckoning. By adding and subtracting, in a sense Hobbes could not possibly have anticipated but which is, structurally, exactly what he described. There are now serious forecasts that artificial general intelligence — machines that reason as flexibly as humans do — will exist within a handful of years. Trillions of dollars are being invested on that assumption. And the philosophical ancestor of all of it is a man in the seventeenth century who wrote three words that he probably didn't fully understand.

One more. In 1913, H.G. Wells read Frederick Soddy's book on radium and learned that radioactive decay releases energy — slowly, over thousands of years, but in total an enormous amount. Wells wrote a novel called The World Set Free in which he imagined accelerating that process. He coined the term "atomic bombs." He got the mechanism completely wrong — his bombs were hand-thrown two-foot spheres that burned continuously rather than detonating. But in 1932, a young Hungarian physicist called Leó Szilard read the novel, and the following year, while crossing a street in London, the idea of the nuclear chain reaction came to him. Szilard later wrote: "Knowing what it would mean — and I knew because I had read H.G. Wells — I did not want this patent to become public."

The fiction caused the physics.

Now, what do all of these have in common? Each of them involves someone taking a framework — atomic theory, mechanism about the mind, radioactive decay — with total seriousness, following its logical implications to an endpoint that sounded insane in their era, getting every mechanistic detail wrong, and turning out to have been pointing at the right kind of thing. The right shape of idea, even if every particular was off.

And here's what I want you to notice: at the time, these ideas were indistinguishable from nonsense. Democritus had exactly as much evidence for atoms as Empedocles had for the four elements, which is to say none. Hobbes's "reasoning is reckoning" was no more or less compelling than dozens of other seventeenth-century claims about the nature of mind that turned out to mean nothing. Wells was writing science fiction. A novel. Entertainment. From the inside, there is no way to tell which speculative extrapolation from contested premises will eventually point at something real and which will end up in the dustbin alongside phlogiston and the luminiferous aether.

So that's what we're going to do here. We're going to work below the floor.

If you've encountered NASA's Technology Readiness Levels, you'll know they run from TRL 1 — basic principles observed and reported — up to TRL 9, which is a fully operational system. Drexler's notion of exploratory engineering sits at around TRL 1 to 2: you take known physics and design hypothetical systems that are beyond current manufacturing but within established physical law. The Alcubierre warp drive is exploratory engineering. Drexler's molecular nanotechnology is exploratory engineering.

What we're doing is below TRL 1. We're at what I call TRL minus one and minus two. At TRL minus one, you have a framework that's internally coherent and taken seriously by some experts, but it's genuinely contested whether it describes reality at all. At TRL minus two, the coherence of the framework itself is uncertain — you're not sure the concepts even refer to anything. This is Democritus territory. This is Hobbes territory. This is a chemist in 1810 who isn't even sure atoms are real, wondering if you could release their binding energy.

We're going to take frameworks — from physics, from philosophy of mind, from theology, from metaphysics, from the philosophy of fiction — and we're going to treat their axioms as engineering specifications. Not as things to argue about in the abstract, but as things to build with. We're going to ask: if this framework is true, what can you exploit? Where are the vulnerabilities? What does the system permit that its designers didn't intend?

Most of what we come up with will be nonsense. I need you to be comfortable with that. The hit rate for this kind of thinking is perhaps one in a hundred over a career, one in a thousand over a generation, one in ten thousand over a century. But when it hits — atoms, computation, nuclear energy — it defines civilisations.

And here's the last thing I want to leave you with tonight.

Charted intellectual lineage from ancient shamans through nuclear weapons development
Charted intellectual lineage from ancient shamans through nuclear weapons development
Heraclitus would have been sitting by a hearth — a Greek domestic hearth, the hestia, which was both a piece of furniture and a goddess. In a well-off Ephesian household around 500 BCE, it would have been a raised stone or clay platform in the centre of the main room, burning olive wood or charcoal, with the smoke rising through an opening in the roof. The fire was never supposed to go out. It was sacred to Hestia. Every meal began with an offering thrown into it. When he wrote that "this world-order, the same of all, no god nor man did create, but it ever was and is and will be: ever-living fire, kindling in measures and being quenched in measures," he was probably looking at exactly that fire — the one in the middle of his house that had been burning, in principle, since the house was built.

Here's the path. One continuous thread.

Someone whose name we will never know sits on open ground somewhere in what is now southern Africa, perhaps 70,000 years ago, perhaps more. They have language, or something close to it. They have fire — their own fire, the one they made or carried. They look up. The sky is extravagant with stars in a way that no living human in an industrialised country has ever seen. And they notice that the stars are not like fire. Fire flickers, gutters, dies. The stars don't. They return to the same positions. They follow paths. Whatever is up there is steady and permanent in a way that nothing down here is. The thought doesn't form as a proposition. It forms as a feeling — a sense that the stuff up there is different from the stuff down here, more powerful, more enduring. The feeling doesn't go anywhere. It can't. There's no framework to put it in. It's a dream at the edge of cognition, and it dissolves by morning.

Thousands of years pass. The feeling persists in fragments — in myths about celestial fire, in the identification of gods with stars, in the Vedic hymns to Agni where fire on Earth and fire in the sky are manifestations of the same divine substance. These aren't theories. They're poetry. But the poetry keeps circling the same intuition: what's up there and what's down here are somehow the same kind of thing.

Heraclitus, sitting by his hearth in Ephesus around 500 BCE, makes the intuition explicit. Everything is fire. Not metaphorically — fire is the arche, the fundamental substance. The cosmos is an ever-living fire, kindling and being quenched in measures. The stars burn with the same fire that burns in his hearth, and that fire is the logos, the rational principle that governs all transformation. He's wrong about almost everything, but he's done something extraordinary: he's taken the campfire intuition and turned it into a claim about the unity of nature. The stuff up there and the stuff down here really are the same stuff, governed by the same principles. That's TRL -3 crystallising into words for the first time.

A generation or two later, Democritus in Abdera takes a different path to a related place. Not fire but atoms — indivisible particles moving through void. Everything is atoms. The stars are atoms. Your body is atoms. The differences between things are differences in arrangement, not substance. He has no evidence. He's reasoning from first principles: change requires combination, combination requires parts, parts must bottom out somewhere. But the implication is profound and nobody notices it for two millennia: if everything is made of the same discrete units, and those units are held together by some binding principle, then the energy locked in that binding is a feature of all matter, not just special matter. The stars and the stones are the same stuff arranged differently. Whatever makes the stars burn is in the stones too, in principle. Democritus can't see that implication because he doesn't have the concept of energy. But it's there, latent in his axioms.

Aristotle buries atomism for a long time. His four elements — earth, water, air, fire — and his distinction between the terrestrial and celestial realms actively prevent the insight from developing. The stuff up there (the quintessence, the fifth element) is explicitly not the same as the stuff down here. The heavens are perfect and unchanging; the sublunary world is imperfect and subject to decay. This is a regression. The campfire intuition — that it's all the same stuff — is ruled out by the dominant framework for nearly two thousand years.

Aristarchus of Samos, around 270 BCE, proposes that the Earth goes around the Sun. If true, this implies the stars are enormously far away (otherwise you'd see parallax). If they're enormously far away and still visible, they must be enormously luminous — possibly like the Sun. Possibly suns themselves. This is a crucial step, but it's ignored. Ptolemy's geocentric model, which keeps the stars on a nearby sphere, wins.

Fast forward. Copernicus, 1543, publishes De Revolutionibus. The Sun is at the centre. But he keeps the stellar sphere — the stars are still fixed to an outer shell. The stars still aren't physical objects at physical distances.

Thomas Digges, 1576, removes the shell. He publishes a diagram showing the stars scattered through infinite space, not attached to anything. For the first time in the Western tradition, the stars are individual physical objects at individual physical distances. "What makes them glow" becomes a question that could in principle have a physical answer. But nobody has any idea what that answer might be. Every fuel anyone knows about — wood, coal, oil, tallow — would burn out in centuries at most. The Sun and stars have been burning for what appears to be forever. The question is legitimate now, but it doesn't connect to anything actionable. TRL -2.5.

Giordano Bruno, around the same time, goes further: the stars are suns, possibly with their own planets. He's burned at the stake in 1600, partly for this.

Newton, 1687, gives us universal gravitation — the same force that pulls an apple down holds the Moon in orbit and the planets around the Sun. This is Heraclitus's unity principle made mathematical. The stuff up there really does obey the same laws as the stuff down here. But Newton says nothing about what fuels the stars. He doesn't have the physics for it. Nobody does.

William Herschel, in the late 1700s, catalogues thousands of stars and nebulae. The universe is vast, full of objects, and governed by knowable laws. But the energy source remains completely mysterious.

John Dalton, 1808, publishes his atomic theory. Chemicals react in fixed whole-number ratios. The simplest explanation: matter comes in discrete units with fixed masses. Democritus, vindicated after 2,200 years. But it's contested — Ernst Mach will argue that atoms are a useful fiction, not real entities, as late as the 1890s. Still, if atoms are real, something holds them together. And whatever that is, it must involve enormous force relative to the atom's size, because atoms are extraordinarily stable under every chemical condition anyone has ever observed. The logical step — enormous binding energy implies potential for enormous release — is available to anyone who takes atomism seriously. But it's speculative philosophy, not science. The mechanism is completely unspecified. TRL -2.

Julius Robert von Mayer, James Prescott Joule, and Hermann von Helmholtz, in the 1840s, establish the conservation of energy. Energy can be converted from one form to another but never created or destroyed. This is essential scaffolding: it means that whatever makes the stars shine must be converting something into light and heat, and the total energy budget must balance. Lord Kelvin and Helmholtz propose gravitational contraction — the Sun is slowly shrinking, converting gravitational potential energy into heat. This gives the Sun a lifetime of about 20-30 million years. Geologists and Darwin's theory of evolution require much longer. Something doesn't add up.

Dmitri Mendeleev, 1869, publishes the periodic table. Elements aren't just different-sized atoms — they have a deep, regular structure. The regularity implies that atoms have internal structure, which implies that the "indivisible" particles of Democritus are actually composite. If they're composite, they can in principle be taken apart. If they can be taken apart, the forces holding them together could in principle be released.

Henri Becquerel, 1896, discovers radioactivity. A uranium salt left on a photographic plate fogs the plate in the dark. Something is coming out of the uranium — energy, spontaneously, without any input. Marie and Pierre Curie isolate radium and polonium. Radium is astonishingly energetic — it glows in the dark, it heats up, it keeps going for years. The energy is coming from inside the atom. The campfire intuition — that the stuff up there and the stuff down here contain the same hidden power — is now an observed phenomenon. TRL -1.

Ernest Rutherford and Frederick Soddy, 1903, work out that radioactive decay involves the transmutation of one element into another. Atoms are literally changing their identity, and releasing energy as they do so. Soddy calculates the total energy: a gram of radium, over its lifetime, releases enough energy to lift a battleship. He writes publicly about transforming deserts, thawing the poles, creating paradise on Earth. He also sees the other side. The energy locked in ordinary matter, if released, would be devastating beyond comprehension.

H.G. Wells reads Soddy's The Interpretation of Radium (1909) and writes The World Set Free (1913, published 1914). He coins the term "atomic bombs." His bombs are wrong in every detail — hand-thrown two-foot spheres of a fictional element called Carolinum that burn continuously rather than detonating. But the core logic is right: atomic energy, released suddenly, would be a weapon beyond anything in human history. He dedicates the book to Soddy. TRL -1 for the application; TRL 1 for the underlying physics.

Albert Einstein, 1905, publishes the special theory of relativity and derives E=mc². Mass and energy are interchangeable. A tiny amount of mass corresponds to an enormous amount of energy. This is the quantitative answer to "what makes the stars burn" — they're converting mass into energy. The campfire sage's intuition is now an equation. It also tells you exactly how much energy is locked inside ordinary matter. The answer is terrifying.

Ernest Rutherford, 1911, discovers the atomic nucleus. Most of the atom is empty space; almost all the mass is concentrated in a tiny core. The binding energy of the nucleus is what holds it together, and that binding energy, per nucleon, is described by a curve. Some nuclei are more tightly bound than others. If you could rearrange nucleons from a less tightly bound configuration to a more tightly bound one, the difference in binding energy would be released. This is the physics that will eventually underpin both fission and fusion. But nobody has a mechanism for doing it.

Arthur Eddington, 1920, proposes that the Sun is powered by nuclear fusion — hydrogen being converted into helium deep in its core, with the mass difference released as energy via E=mc². The stars burn with nuclear fire. Heraclitus's ever-living fire, kindling in measures and being quenched in measures, is a startlingly accurate description of stellar nucleosynthesis, where the balance between gravitational collapse and radiation pressure produces a star that burns steadily for billions of years, "kindling and quenching" in a self-regulating equilibrium. He didn't know. He couldn't have known. But he was pointing at the right thing.

James Chadwick, 1932, discovers the neutron. This is the key. Neutrons have no electric charge, so they can penetrate the nucleus without being repelled by the protons' positive charge. They're the lockpick that can get inside the atom.

Leó Szilard, September 12, 1933, crosses a street in London, and the idea of the nuclear chain reaction comes to him. One neutron hits a nucleus. The nucleus splits. Two neutrons come out. Each hits another nucleus. Four neutrons. Eight. Sixteen. Exponential. He files a patent in 1934. He tries beryllium — it doesn't chain. He tries indium — it doesn't chain. He doesn't know which element will work. But the concept is correct, specific, and novel. TRL 0.

Enrico Fermi, 1934, bombards uranium with neutrons in Rome and observes strange results. He thinks he's creating new transuranic elements. He's actually splitting the uranium nucleus — achieving fission — but he doesn't realise it. The discovery is there in his data, unrecognised for four years.

Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann, December 1938, in Berlin, bombard uranium with neutrons and find barium in the products. Barium has roughly half the atomic number of uranium. The uranium is splitting in half. Hahn writes to his former collaborator Lise Meitner, now in exile in Sweden because she's Jewish. "Perhaps you can suggest some fantastic explanation," he writes.

Lise Meitner and her nephew Otto Frisch, walking in the snow in Kungälv, Sweden, over Christmas 1938, work out the physics. The uranium nucleus, heavy and unstable, is deforming like a liquid drop when hit by a neutron. It elongates, necks, splits. The two fragments repel each other with enormous force — the electrostatic repulsion of fifty-odd protons in each fragment. The energy released matches E=mc² applied to the mass difference. Frisch coins the term "fission," borrowed from biology (cell division). Within weeks, multiple labs confirm the result. The neutron yield per fission is greater than one. A chain reaction is physically possible. TRL 1. Basic principles observed and reported.

Niels Bohr and John Archibald Wheeler, 1939, work out theoretically that it's uranium-235 (the rare isotope, 0.7% of natural uranium) that fissions readily, not uranium-238. This means you need isotope separation — an enormous engineering challenge — to make a bomb.

Albert Einstein and Leó Szilard write to President Roosevelt, August 2, 1939. "It may become possible to set up a nuclear chain reaction in a large mass of uranium, by which vast amounts of power and large quantities of new radium-like elements would be generated. This new phenomenon would also lead to the construction of bombs." TRL 2: concept formulated, application identified, speculative.

Enrico Fermi and his team build Chicago Pile-1 under the stands of the University of Chicago's Stagg Field. On December 2, 1942, at 3:25 PM, the control rods are withdrawn. The neutron count rises steadily, exponentially, exactly as Szilard had predicted nine years earlier. The chain reaction is self-sustaining. Fermi lets it run for 28 minutes. Arthur Compton calls James Conant: "The Italian navigator has landed in the New World." "How were the natives?" "Very friendly." TRL 3: proof of concept.

Ernest Lawrence at Berkeley develops electromagnetic isotope separation (the calutron). Gaseous diffusion plants rise at Oak Ridge, Tennessee. Plutonium production reactors are built at Hanford, Washington — each one a scaled-up version of Fermi's pile, designed by Eugene Wigner, cooled by the Columbia River. At Los Alamos, Robert Oppenheimer assembles the most extraordinary concentration of scientific talent in history. Seth Neddermeyer and George Kistiakowsky develop the implosion lens that will compress a plutonium sphere to supercritical density. Hans Bethe runs the theoretical division. Edward Teller keeps pushing for the "Super" — the hydrogen bomb — and has to be pulled back to the immediate task. TRL 4-5: components and subsystems being validated.

July 16, 1945, 5:29 AM Mountain War Time. The Jornada del Muerto desert in New Mexico. The gadget — an implosion-type plutonium device — sits atop a 100-foot steel tower. Norris Bradbury supervised the final assembly. The countdown reaches zero. For a fraction of a microsecond, the temperature at the core reaches tens of millions of degrees — the temperature of stellar interiors. Heraclitus's ever-living fire burns on Earth for the first time. The yield is 21 kilotons. The steel tower evaporates. The sand beneath it fuses into a glassy mineral that will be named trinitite. The fireball is visible from 200 miles. Oppenheimer recalls the Bhagavad Gita: "Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds." Kenneth Bainbridge, the test director, says something more honest: "Now we are all sons of bitches." TRL 6: system prototype demonstrated in relevant environment.

August 6, 1945. The B-29 Enola Gay, piloted by Colonel Paul Tibbets, drops Little Boy — a uranium gun-type bomb, 64 kilograms of uranium-235 — on Hiroshima. 80,000 people die instantly. August 9. Bockscar drops Fat Man — a plutonium implosion device — on Nagasaki. 40,000 dead. TRL 7-8: system demonstrated in operational environment.

Then the arms race. The Soviet Union tests Joe-1 in August 1949 — a near-copy of Fat Man, built with intelligence from Klaus Fuchs. Truman authorises the hydrogen bomb programme. Edward Teller and Stanislaw Ulam devise the Teller-Ulam design — radiation implosion, staging, the trick that makes thermonuclear weapons possible. Ivy Mike, November 1, 1952, Enewetak Atoll — 10.4 megatons, the first true thermonuclear detonation. The island of Elugelab ceases to exist. Castle Bravo, March 1, 1954 — 15 megatons, two and a half times the expected yield. The crew of the Japanese fishing vessel Lucky Dragon No. 5 is showered with radioactive fallout 80 miles away. Tsar Bomba, October 30, 1961, Novaya Zemlya — 50 megatons, the largest nuclear detonation in history. The fireball is five miles across. The mushroom cloud rises 40 miles. Windows break 560 miles away. The shockwave circles the Earth three times. Andrei Sakharov, who helped design it, becomes a peace activist. TRL 9: operational systems, proven through successful deployment, fully integrated into military doctrine.

And then the final product — the thing that actually shapes the world you live in — is something that the campfire sage couldn't have parsed as a concept at all. It's not the bomb. The bomb is just the mechanism. The product is deterrence. Mutually assured destruction. Second-strike capability. Submarine-launched ballistic missiles cruising silently beneath the ocean, each carrying enough warheads to end civilisation, their locations unknown, their purpose to never be used. The hotline between Washington and Moscow. The doctrine of flexible response. The Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty. The reason Ukraine doesn't invade Poland, the reason the Cold War stayed cold, the reason there's been no great power war since 1945 — all of it downstream of a nameless human sitting by a fire 70,000 years ago and feeling, without words for it, that the stuff up there was the same kind of thing as the stuff down here.

One continuous path. One idea, climbing the ladder from fairy tale to physics, from myth to engineering, from campfire to Tsar Bomba. Every step looking like nonsense from the step below. Every step, in retrospect, inevitable.

I think what you're asking for is the phenomenology at each step — not just who had the idea, but what it felt like to have it, and why everyone around them thought it was nonsense. Let me do this properly.

Somewhere in what we now call southern France or northern Spain, perhaps 40,000 years ago, perhaps earlier. The people who live here paint. They paint horses and bison and hands on the walls of caves in firelight, which means they understand representation — that a thing can stand for another thing, that a mark on a wall can mean an animal that isn't there. They have figurative art, which means they have metaphor, which means they have the cognitive architecture to think "this is like that" even when the two things are very different.

One of them — a woman, a man, it doesn't matter, their name is gone — is sitting outside at night. The fire they made is burning. The sky is doing what it always does. And the thought forms, or almost forms. It's not in words as we'd understand them. It's more like a rhyme between two perceptions — the flickering thing here that they made and the steady things up there that nobody made. The warmth that comes off the fire and the cold light that comes off the stars. They're both... bright? They both... glow? Is the sky... burning?

And the thought stalls, because it doesn't work. Fire is a thing you feed. You put wood in and it eats the wood and it gives you warmth and light and then the wood is gone and the fire goes out. The stars don't go out. Nobody feeds them. They don't eat anything. They're not fire. The two things rhyme but they don't connect. It's like seeing a face in a rock — the pattern is there but the meaning isn't. The thought is a feeling with no structure to hang on, and it dissolves.

If they tried to express it — and this is the crucial thing — anyone listening would not disagree. They wouldn't say "no, I think the stars are cold" or "no, the stars run on something else." They would simply not understand what was being said. The utterance would have the same status as saying "what if we could walk into a dream" or "what if the river remembered yesterday." It's not wrong. It's not a claim that can be evaluated. It's a category confusion — treating the sky as if it were the same kind of thing as a hearth. It's poetry at best. A child's question. You smile and move on.

And yet. The stars really do contain fire. The rhyme between the campfire and the starlight really is pointing at something. But the something is so far from anything this person can access that the thought is genuinely inexpressible — not because they lack vocabulary, but because they lack every single intermediate concept between "things glow" and "thermonuclear fusion converts hydrogen to helium via the proton-proton chain at temperatures of fifteen million kelvin."

Heraclitus, Ephesus, around 500 BCE. He's not at a campfire. He's at a hestia — the hearth at the centre of his house. It's a raised clay or stone platform, probably burning olive wood or charcoal, and it's been alight continuously since the house was established, because the hearth fire is sacred to the goddess Hestia. Every Greek household keeps the hearth burning. When a new colony is founded, they carry fire from the mother city's public hearth to light the new one. Fire is civilisation's thread. It connects past to present, mother city to daughter city, the human world to the divine.

Heraclitus looks at this fire and sees something nobody else sees. Not just warmth, not just the sacred flame, but the principle of all change. Everything flows, everything transforms, and fire is the purest expression of transformation — it consumes fuel and becomes something else, endlessly. He proposes that fire is the arche, the fundamental substance. "This world-order, the same of all, no god nor man did create, but it ever was and is and will be: ever-living fire, kindling in measures and being quenched in measures."

This is radical. He's saying the same fire that burns in his hearth is the same principle that burns in the stars, and that this principle is what everything is made of, and that the universe itself is a fire that kindles and quenches in a self-regulating rhythm.

His contemporaries think he's obscure and probably mad. They call him "the Obscure" and "the Weeping Philosopher." Aristotle later says he probably didn't understand his own words. The claim that everything is fire seems like exactly the kind of grandiose metaphorical hand-waving that philosophers do when they haven't done the actual work of careful observation. Empedocles has four elements, Thales had water, Anaximenes had air — why fire? What's the argument? There isn't one, really. It's an intuition dressed in aphorism. The other philosophers have equally compelling intuitions.

And yet. "Ever-living fire, kindling in measures and being quenched in measures" is a startlingly accurate description of a main-sequence star, where the balance between gravitational collapse (quenching) and radiation pressure from nuclear fusion (kindling) produces a self-regulating equilibrium that burns steadily for billions of years. He didn't know. He couldn't have known. But the shape of his intuition — that fire is a self-regulating process, not a substance; that it kindles and quenches in measures; that it's the same fire everywhere — maps onto stellar physics with eerie precision.

Democritus, Abdera, around 400 BCE. A different path to a related destination. He's not interested in fire specifically. He's interested in the structure of everything. His starting point is philosophical, not observational: if you cut something in half, and cut the halves in half, you must eventually reach a piece that can't be cut further. Otherwise, matter is infinitely divisible, which leads to paradoxes (Zeno's paradoxes are in the air). So there must be atoms — atomos, uncuttable things — and the void they move through. Everything is atoms and void. The stars, the sea, your body, a stone. All the same atoms, differently arranged.

He has no evidence. None. He has never observed an atom. His atoms are a solution to a logical problem, not an empirical discovery. He imagines they come in different shapes — some have hooks, some are smooth, some interlock. Fire atoms are small and round, which is why fire flows and penetrates everything.

Nobody takes this very seriously. Aristotle rejects atomism comprehensively, and Aristotle wins the argument for two millennia. The idea that there's void — literal nothingness, empty space — is considered logically absurd by most Greek philosophers. The idea that the fundamental constituents of reality are invisible, indivisible particles in random motion seems like a bizarre speculation with no way to confirm or deny it.

What nobody notices — because they can't, because they don't have the concept of energy — is that if atoms are real, they contain something. Whatever holds them together as discrete, stable units involves force. And force implies energy. And the fact that atoms are extraordinarily stable — that nothing in ordinary chemistry can break them apart — implies that the energy holding them together is enormous compared to anything accessible through normal chemical processes. The logical chain from "atoms are real" to "there's enormous energy locked inside matter" is available in principle. But nobody runs the chain because the intermediate concepts don't exist yet.

I'm going to compress the long middle stretch where the idea is mostly dormant — the centuries where Aristotle's framework dominates and atomism is a footnote — and pick up where it reawakens.

Robert Boyle, 1661. The Sceptical Chymist. He attacks the Aristotelian four-element theory and argues that elements are the simplest substances that chemicals can be decomposed into. He doesn't endorse atomism directly, but he starts thinking about matter in terms of small "corpuscles" with specific properties. More importantly, he establishes the idea that matter is conserved in chemical reactions — you get out what you put in, rearranged. This is essential groundwork. If matter is conserved, then whatever changes during a chemical reaction isn't the matter itself but its arrangement. And if arrangement can change while matter stays the same, then the forces governing arrangement are separate from the matter they act on. Forces and matter are different things.

Antoine Lavoisier, 1789. The conservation of mass in chemical reactions is established rigorously. Nothing is created or destroyed, only transformed. This is the quantitative backbone: if you track everything carefully, the books balance. But it also raises a question that Lavoisier doesn't pursue: if the books always balance, then energy must be accounted for too. When something burns, the fire has to come from somewhere and go somewhere.

John Dalton, 1808. Atomic theory, finally, with evidence. Chemicals react in fixed whole-number ratios (the law of multiple proportions). The simplest explanation: matter comes in discrete units with fixed masses, and chemical reactions involve rearranging these units. Hydrogen atoms combine with oxygen atoms in fixed ratios to make water. This isn't philosophy anymore. It's chemistry. The atoms have specific, measurable relative weights.

But it's contested. Seriously contested. Many leading scientists — Ernst Mach most prominently, but also Wilhelm Ostwald and Pierre Duhem — argue that atoms are a useful calculating device, not real things. You can do all the chemistry you need with "equivalent weights" without committing to the actual physical existence of tiny indivisible particles. The debate won't be fully settled until Einstein's 1905 paper on Brownian motion, nearly a century later.

So if you're a chemist in, say, 1820, and you take Dalton seriously, and you think atoms are real, you can reason as follows: atoms are extraordinarily stable. Nothing in chemistry breaks them apart — reactions rearrange atoms but never destroy them. Whatever holds an atom together must therefore involve a force that is enormously stronger than any chemical force, because chemical forces are what we use to pull things apart and they can't touch atoms. And if there's an enormous force, there's enormous energy, because force over distance is energy.

Could you release it?

The question is available. The logic is sound. But to anyone around you, the question is meaningless. First, you're not even sure atoms are real. Second, even if they are, "releasing their binding energy" is a sentence without a referent — you have no idea what kind of force is involved, no mechanism for accessing it, no way to even begin. It's like asking "could you release the binding energy of love" — it uses words that separately mean something but don't add up to a proposition. You'd be laughed at. Not because it's wrong, but because it's not even coherent enough to be wrong.

Julius Robert von Mayer, 1842. James Prescott Joule, 1843. Hermann von Helmholtz, 1847. The conservation of energy is established across three independent lines of work.

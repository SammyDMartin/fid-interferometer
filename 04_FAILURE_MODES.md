# Failure Modes and Quality Criteria

## The Failed Gödel Attempt: A Case Study

During this conversation, Claude attempted to generate an ontological engineering proposal and failed. The attempt and the diagnosis of why it failed are instructive.

### The Attempt
"The Incompleteness Exploit" — claimed that if the universe IS a mathematical structure (Tegmark's MUH), then Gödel's incompleteness theorems prove an "outside" must exist, and singularities might be "Gödel points" where the formal system hits its own incompleteness boundaries.

### Why It Failed
Sammy's diagnosis: "If physics is a formal system, that leads to contradictions. Naked singularities are not there because something evades the formal system's completeness. They're there because the laws just — various things just diverge in a way that is completely provable. There's even the singularity theorems."

The core error: **singularities are derived WITHIN GR using GR's own formalism.** The Penrose-Hawking singularity theorems PROVE singularities exist as consequences of the theory. This is the opposite of a Gödel sentence, which is something a system CANNOT prove. The proposal noticed a surface resemblance ("both involve the system breaking down") and treated it as a logical connection. It wasn't. It was a colourful metaphor disguised as an inference.

Classification: TRL -3 at best. "The stars contain fire" level — pattern-matching on superficial similarity without mechanistic connection.

### Key Lesson
The most dangerous failure mode is producing something that **sounds** like a valid proposal — uses formal language, references real theorems, draws connections between established concepts — but is actually just an analogy dressed up in technical vocabulary. The test is: does the conclusion **follow** from the premises by logical inference, or does it merely **resemble** something that follows?

## Failure Mode Taxonomy

### 1. Colourful Metaphor Disguised as Inference (Most Common)
Noticing that two phenomena share a surface feature and treating the resemblance as a logical connection.

**Signature:** "X involves limits/breakdown/infinity. Y also involves limits/breakdown/infinity. Therefore X and Y are connected."

**Test:** Can you write out the logical steps from premise to conclusion? If any step requires "this is like..." or "this resembles..." rather than "therefore..." or "it follows that...", you have a metaphor, not an inference.

### 2. Framework Mixing Without Logical Bridge
Combining concepts from two different frameworks without establishing why the combination is valid within either framework.

**Signature:** "Framework A says X. Framework B says Y. Therefore X+Y."

**Test:** Would a proponent of Framework A accept the conclusion? Would a proponent of Framework B? If neither would, you haven't exploited either framework — you've just juxtaposed them.

### 3. Equivocation on Technical Terms
Using a word that has a precise meaning in one context with a different meaning in another context, and treating them as the same.

**Signature:** "Singularities are where the system 'breaks down.' Gödel sentences are where the system 'breaks down.' Therefore singularities are Gödel sentences." (The word "breaks down" means completely different things in each case.)

### 4. Vagueness Masquerading as Generality
Making a claim so general that it's unfalsifiable and therefore uninformative.

**Signature:** "At the deepest level, everything is connected." This is TRL -3. It might be true, but it doesn't tell you anything specific enough to be an engineering specification.

### 5. Reversed Burden of Proof
Treating the inability to disprove something as evidence for it.

**Signature:** "You can't prove this WON'T work, therefore it might work." Valid ontological engineering proposals don't rely on this — they show that the conclusion FOLLOWS from premises the framework already accepts.

## Quality Criteria for Valid Proposals

A genuine ontological engineering proposal must satisfy ALL of the following:

1. **Identifies a specific framework** with stated axioms/premises
2. **The premises are ones the framework's own proponents accept** (not strawmen)
3. **The conclusion follows by valid logical inference** (not analogy or resemblance)
4. **The conclusion is surprising** — the framework's proponents haven't noticed it or have actively avoided it
5. **The conclusion sounds insane** but cannot be dismissed without rejecting one of the accepted premises
6. **The proposal is specific enough** to be wrong — it makes a claim about what would happen under specified conditions
7. **It identifies a mechanism or logical chain**, however speculative — not just an observation of similarity

## What It Feels Like To Generate One

Based on Sammy Martin's experience generating seven proposals over several years:

- They're rare. Most people produce zero in a lifetime.
- They require holding multiple frameworks simultaneously in your head (physics, philosophy, theology, fiction, anthropic reasoning)
- They arrive as sudden insights, not as products of systematic search
- You're never sure if you have a genuine insight or a sophisticated-sounding non-sequitur
- The ones that work have a specific feeling: "wait, does the framework actually imply this? That can't be right. But I can't find where the logic breaks..."
- If you can immediately see why it works, it's probably not novel enough. If you can immediately see why it fails, it's probably a metaphor. The good ones sit in the uncomfortable middle.

Synthesized quantum computing concepts with matrix multiplication applications

arXiv
. And there's work using "hyperparallel" quantum computing with hyperentangled states that achieves O(N²) time complexity for matrix multiplication 
Nature
. So on paper, yes — quantum speedups for matrix multiplication exist.

The problem is a brutal one though, and it cuts right at the AI application. There's a November 2025 survey paper with the telling title "Quantum Deep Learning Still Needs a Quantum Leap" that lays out three obstacles:

First, the theoretical speedups in operation count are overwhelmed on practical problem sizes by how slowly quantum computers perform each operation compared to classical hardware — particularly GPUs, which have been improving rapidly for parallel computation 
arXiv
. A quantum computer might need fewer operations to multiply two matrices, but each operation is orders of magnitude slower than a GPU clock cycle, and you need massive error correction overhead on top.

Second, many promising quantum algorithms depend on practical Quantum Random Access Memory (QRAM), which is underdeveloped 
arXiv
. You need to get the classical matrix data into the quantum system efficiently, and getting it back out again. This is the input/output bottleneck — you can't just feed a 175-billion-parameter weight matrix into a quantum computer. The encoding and readout costs can eat the entire speedup.

Third — and this is the really interesting part — there's Ewin Tang's dequantization work. Tang, as an undergraduate supervised by Aaronson, was originally tasked with proving that no fast classical algorithm existed for a quantum ML problem. Instead she found classical algorithms that matched the quantum speedups by using sampling techniques inspired by the quantum algorithms themselves 
Wikipedia
. This generalised: her framework showed that for essentially all known quantum linear algebra techniques, when the quantum algorithm assumes a certain data structure for input, a classical algorithm with sampling access to that same structure can match the quantum performance 
arXiv
. So many of the claimed exponential quantum speedups for ML turned out to be artefacts of unfair comparisons — the quantum algorithm was being compared to naive classical algorithms rather than clever classical algorithms using similar tricks.

So where does that leave things? The honest answer is: quantum computing probably won't speed up AI training in any foreseeable timeframe, and it might never do so for the specific workload of dense matrix multiplication on classical data. The speedups that exist are either polynomial (not exponential), require hardware that doesn't exist (QRAM, fault-tolerant logical qubits at massive scale), or have been partially dequantized.

The places where quantum computing might eventually matter for AI-adjacent problems are more niche: simulating quantum systems (relevant for drug discovery, materials science), certain optimisation problems, and possibly some specialised linear algebra tasks where the data is already quantum in nature. But "make transformer training faster" is probably not in the cards.

Identified Deutsch algorithm as simplest quantum speedup example
Identified Deutsch algorithm as simplest quantum speedup example
The Deutsch algorithm. It's the absolute simplest quantum speedup, and it's provable — not just "we don't know a classical method," but "no classical method can exist."

The setup: someone gives you a black-box function f that takes one bit in and spits one bit out. There are four possibilities — f(0)=0 and f(1)=0, or f(0)=1 and f(1)=1, or f(0)=0 and f(1)=1, or f(0)=1 and f(1)=0. The first two are "constant" (same output regardless of input), the last two are "balanced" (different outputs). Your task: determine whether f is constant or balanced.

Classically, no matter what kind of system you use — digital, analog, resistor network, mechanical contraption, anything — you have to evaluate f twice. Once on 0, once on 1, then compare. There's no way around this. The function is a black box. You can't infer what f(1) does from f(0). Two queries, minimum. This isn't a limitation of digital computing or sequential architecture. It's an information-theoretic fact about black-box functions.

The quantum algorithm does it in one query. You prepare a superposition, apply f once (as a quantum oracle, which is a unitary that encodes f), and then a Hadamard and measurement tells you constant vs balanced. One evaluation of f. The trick is that the oracle gets applied to a superposition of both inputs simultaneously, and the interference pattern after the final Hadamard is different for constant versus balanced — constructive interference pushes you to |0⟩ for constant, destructive interference pushes you to |1⟩ for balanced.

One query instead of two. No classical system can do this. Not because we haven't been clever enough, but because classically a black-box function simply must be evaluated on each input separately to learn about each input. The quantum system evaluates it on a superposition of inputs and extracts a global property of f from the interference, without ever learning what f does on either individual input.

The Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm is the scaled-up version: n-bit input, function is either constant or balanced, and classically you need up to 2^(n-1) + 1 queries in the worst case. The quantum algorithm still does it in one. That's an exponential separation.

Option 1: "The superposition is just epistemic — it represents our ignorance." If the qubit isn't really in both states, if it's just that we don't know which state it's in, then there's no mechanism for interference. The oracle was applied to one definite input, we just don't know which one. But then the algorithm shouldn't work. Classical ignorance about which input was queried cannot produce a deterministic answer about the relationship between both outputs. If the function was only evaluated on one input, the output can only contain information about that input. The algorithm works. So this option is just wrong.

Option 2: "The superposition is real in some operational sense, but don't ask what's happening between measurements." This is the standard Copenhagen move. The wavefunction evolves unitarily, the oracle acts on the superposition, the Hadamard creates the interference, and the measurement gives the right answer. The formalism predicts the outcome perfectly. But if you ask "was the function evaluated on both inputs?" the answer is: that question is meaningless. There is no fact of the matter about what happened between preparation and measurement. Only measurement outcomes are real.

But this is exactly the dodge that makes Copenhagen frustrating here. Because the reason the algorithm works — the mechanism — is that the oracle was applied to a superposition of both inputs and the results interfered. That's not an interpretation, that's the calculation. If you refuse to say that's what "really happened," then you have no explanation for why the right answer comes out. You have a formalism that gives the right prediction and a principled refusal to say what the formalism is describing. Which means you're saying: yes, the algorithm does something that looks logically impossible from a classical standpoint, and no, I won't explain how, but it works.

But now you're saying something physically happened on two inputs at once, and the results combined through interference to produce the output. Which is... many-worlds minus the honesty about what "both things happened" means. You're saying both evaluations occurred, both contributed to the outcome, but somehow there's still only one world. Where did the other evaluation happen? In the one world? Simultaneously? Then what does "one world" even mean for those few microseconds?

This is the option where Copenhagen starts sounding like many-worlds with extra steps and a vague prohibition on taking your own formalism seriously.

Sort of. What it gives you is a choice between: the algorithm works and I refuse to explain why (option 2), or the algorithm works because both evaluations happened and I refuse to follow that thought to its conclusion (option 3). Neither is a logical contradiction in the strict sense — Copenhagen is carefully constructed to never make a falsifiable ontological claim that could be contradicted. But it achieves this by declining to have an ontology. The "just accept it" isn't "accept this contradiction." It's "accept that asking why it works is not a legitimate question." Which, depending on your philosophical temperament, is either admirably disciplined or an intellectual cop-out dressed up as rigour.

From the Everettian perspective, the answer to "how did one query give you information about both inputs?" is simple and has no loose ends: both queries happened, in different branches, and the branches interfered before decohering. Logic is intact. Nothing impossible occurred. You learned one bit, the other evaluations' results are in branches you can't access. The √N speedup is real, and Grover's does generalise to any search problem. But there are a few things that make it much less useful in practice than it looks.

First, the oracle isn't free. In our demo, the "is the prize in box 5?" check was a tiny circuit I built by hand — a few X gates and a Toffoli. You have to implement the entire problem specification as reversible quantum gates. For a useful combinatorial problem, that oracle circuit might need thousands or millions of gates, and every gate adds noise. The √N speedup in oracle calls doesn't account for the cost of each call being a deep, noisy circuit.

Second, the √N speedup has to compete against classical hardware that's billions of times faster per operation. A quantum gate takes microseconds. A classical CPU operation takes fractions of a nanosecond. So for Grover's to actually beat a classical brute-force search in wall-clock time, N has to be enormous — large enough that √N × (microseconds per quantum operation) is less than N × (nanoseconds per classical operation). That crossover point is somewhere around N = 10¹⁵ or higher, depending on assumptions. And to search a space of 10¹⁵ items with Grover's, you need roughly 50 qubits running a circuit of depth in the millions — way beyond what NISQ hardware can do without drowning in noise.

Third, √N is... just √N. It's quadratic, not exponential. Doubling the number of qubits in a classical computer squares the search space it can brute-force. Doubling the qubits in a quantum computer also squares the search space, but only gives you a square-root advantage in searching it. For most practical problems, classical heuristics — SAT solvers, branch-and-bound, simulated annealing — do much better than brute-force search anyway. Grover's √N speedup is over brute-force, not over the best classical algorithm. And for most structured problems, the best classical algorithm is far better than brute-force.

So the situation is: Grover's gives you a provable speedup, but it's a quadratic speedup over brute-force, competing against classical heuristics that are often exponentially better than brute-force, running on hardware that's billions of times slower per operation, requiring oracle circuits that are currently impossible to run at useful scale without error correction.

It'll matter someday — on a fault-tolerant machine with millions of physical qubits, Grover's could genuinely accelerate certain cryptographic and optimisation tasks. ## What just happened

The first line of the circuit — circuit.h(i) applied to all six qubits — puts the system into an equal superposition of all 64 possible six-bit strings. Every card in the deck. Every card that isn't in the deck. All at once. Not a probability distribution. Not "we don't know which one." All of them, simultaneously, with equal amplitude.

The theoretical premise
The Finite Improbability Drive, as you've developed it across several papers, works like this:

In the Many-Worlds Interpretation, every quantum measurement splits the universe into branches. All branches are equally real. When you measure a qubit, there's now a branch where it was 0 and a branch where it was 1, and both physically exist.

The quantum suicide thought experiment takes this further: if an observer's survival is conditioned on a quantum outcome — a gun fires or doesn't based on a qubit measurement — then from the observer's perspective, they always survive. Not because the universe conspires to save them, but because there is no experience of the branches where the observer was destroyed. The observer can only find themselves in branches where they still exist. This is anthropic selection applied to quantum branching.

The FID generalises this from survival to any target outcome. Instead of "survive or die," the condition is "desired outcome or observer termination." You create an observer, expose it to quantum randomness, and terminate it in branches where the outcome is unfavourable. The surviving observer finds itself — always, inevitably — in the branch where the improbable thing happened. Not because probability was violated, but because the observer was pruned from all branches where it wasn't.

The key components are: a source of genuine quantum randomness (branch splitting), an observer (something with subjective continuity that it matters to prune), a target outcome (what you want to happen), a sensor (to check whether it happened), and a pruning mechanism (terminate the observer in unfavourable branches). The "finite" version works up to some ceiling of improbability determined by how many branches you can generate and prune. The "infinite" version recursively amplifies this, using the output of one FID as input to build a more powerful one — but we're nowhere near that.
